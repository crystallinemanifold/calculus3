Directions

Read each statement carefully. Circle every true statement and cross out every false one. A problem may have one true statement, several, or all five.

Mark every statement. A statement left unmarked counts as wrong.

Use ○ to circle and ✕ to cross out; click again to undo. The exam has six problems in 35 minutes, so aim for about six minutes per problem. Do a whole problem before checking it.

How to approach each statement

Treat every statement under a problem as its own small question, and do not decide it by how it looks. Each statement is settled by exactly one of four things:

  • Definition such as a Riemann sum, average value, mass, or the Jacobian;
  • Theorem or property, such as Fubini’s theorem, symmetry, comparison, or the change of variables theorem;
  • Formula such as \(dA=r\,dr\,d\theta\), \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\), or a coordinate conversion;
  • Calculation to carry out: sketch the region, find the bounds, evaluate the integral.

Before you circle or cross out, name which one applies, then use it. If it is a calculation, do the calculation.

Example. Problem 1(a) claims \(\displaystyle\iint_R x^2y\,dA=\int_{-1}^{2}x^2\,dx+\int_0^6y\,dy\). That is a claim about a property of double integrals. The theorem that applies: on a rectangle, an integrand of the form \(g(x)h(y)\) splits into the product of two single-variable integrals (Fubini’s theorem). The statement adds them, so it is false. The calculation confirms it: \(3\cdot18=54\), not \(3+18=21\).

Every solution below is labeled with the definition, theorem, formula, or calculation that decides it.

Problems 1–7
  1. 01

    Let \(R\) be the rectangle with vertices \((-1,0)\), \((-1,6)\), \((2,6)\), \((2,0)\), and let \(f(x,y)=x^2y\).

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_R x^2y\,dA=\int_{-1}^{2}x^2\,dx+\int_0^6 y\,dy\).
    2. (b)
      \(\operatorname{Area}(R)=18\).
    3. (c)
      The average value of \(f\) over \(R\) equals the value of \(f\) at the center of \(R\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_R x^2y\,dA=54\).
    5. (e)
      The average value of \(f\) over \(R\) is \(3\).
    Solution to problem 1

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) False. Theorem Fubini's theorem (product integrands)

      A product integrand separates into a product of single integrals, not a sum. The sum is \(3+18=21\), not \(54\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      \(R=[-1,2]\times[0,6]\), so its area is \(3\cdot 6=18\).
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Definition average value

      The center is \(\left(\tfrac12,3\right)\) and \(f\left(\tfrac12,3\right)=\tfrac34\neq 3\). The average value is an integral, not a function value at a convenient point.
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Theorem Fubini's theorem (product integrands)

      The integrand is a product of a function of \(x\) and a function of \(y\), so \(\displaystyle\iint_R x^2y\,dA=\left(\int_{-1}^{2}x^2\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^6 y\,dy\right)=3\cdot 18=54\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Definition average value

      \(\displaystyle f_{\text{ave}}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Area}(R)}\iint_R f\,dA=\frac{54}{18}=3\).
  2. 02

    The following statements concern integrals over rectangles.

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[0,2]\times[0,\pi]} y\sin(xy)\,dA=0\) because sine is an odd function.
    2. (b)
      The volume of the solid under \(z=2y^2-x^2+3\) and above \(R=[-1,1]\times[0,3]\) is \(52\).
    3. (c)
      Divide \([0,4]\times[0,2]\) into \(2\times2\) equal subrectangles. The Riemann sum for \(f(x,y)=x+y^2\) using upper-right corners is \(S_{2,2}=22\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 xye^{x+y}\,dy\,dx=e^2+1\).
    5. (e)
      \(\displaystyle\int_3^4\!\!\int_1^2\frac{dy\,dx}{(2x+y)^2}=\int_3^4\!\!\int_1^2\frac{dx\,dy}{(2x+y)^2}\).
    Solution to problem 2

    Key: two statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) False. Theorem symmetry (odd integrand)

      Symmetry only helps when the region is symmetric about the right line. Here \(x,y\ge 0\), so \(xy\in[0,2\pi]\) and nothing cancels. Integrate in \(x\) first: \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\big[-\cos(xy)\big]_{x=0}^{x=2}dy=\int_0^\pi(1-\cos 2y)\,dy=\pi\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      On \(R\), \(z\ge 2>0\), so the volume is the integral. Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_0^3(2y^2-x^2+3)\,dy=27-3x^2\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(27-3x^2)\,dx=54-2=52\).
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Definition Riemann sum

      The upper-right corners are \((2,1),(2,2),(4,1),(4,2)\), with \(f\)-values \(3,6,5,8\) summing to \(22\). Each must be multiplied by \(\Delta A=2\cdot1=2\), so \(S_{2,2}=44\). (The exact integral is \(80/3\).)
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Theorem Fubini's theorem (product integrands)

      Write \(e^{x+y}=e^xe^y\) and separate: \(\displaystyle\left(\int_0^1xe^x\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^2ye^y\,dy\right)=\big[(x-1)e^x\big]_0^1\cdot\big[(y-1)e^y\big]_0^2=1\cdot(e^2+1)\).
    5. (e)

      (e) False. Theorem Fubini's theorem

      Fubini lets you swap the order, but each pair of limits must travel with its variable. The right side integrates over \(x\in[1,2]\), \(y\in[3,4]\), a different rectangle (\(\approx0.0244\) versus \(\approx0.0141\)). The correct swap is \(\displaystyle\int_1^2\!\!\int_3^4\frac{dx\,dy}{(2x+y)^2}\).
  3. 03

    Let \(R=[0,4]\times[1,3]\) and \(f(x,y)=xy^2\). Divide \(R\) into \(2\times2\) equal subrectangles (two subdivisions in \(x\), two in \(y\)).

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_R xy^2\,dA=\frac{208}{3}\), so the lower-left sum underestimates the integral.
    2. (b)
      The Riemann sum using lower-left corners is \(20\).
    3. (c)
      Each subrectangle has area \(\Delta A=2\).
    4. (d)
      The Riemann sum using upper-right corners is \(78\).
    5. (e)
      The Riemann sum using midpoints is \(68\).
    Solution to problem 3

    Key: four statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Calculation

      \(\displaystyle\left(\int_0^4x\,dx\right)\left(\int_1^3y^2\,dy\right)=8\cdot\frac{26}{3}=\frac{208}{3}\approx69.3\), which is larger than \(20\). (Since \(f\) increases in both \(x\) and \(y\) here, lower-left corners always give the smallest values.)
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      Lower-left corners: \(x\in\{0,2\}\), \(y\in\{1,2\}\). The \(f\)-values are \(0,0,2,8\), which sum to \(10\); times \(\Delta A=2\) gives \(20\).
    3. (c)

      (c) True. Definition Riemann sum

      \(\Delta x=\frac{4-0}{2}=2\) and \(\Delta y=\frac{3-1}{2}=1\), so \(\Delta A=\Delta x\,\Delta y=2\).
    4. (d)

      (d) False. Definition Riemann sum

      Upper-right corners: \(x\in\{2,4\}\), \(y\in\{2,3\}\), with \(f\)-values \(8,18,16,36\) summing to \(78\). A Riemann sum multiplies each value by \(\Delta A=2\), so the sum is \(156\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Calculation

      Midpoints: \(x\in\{1,3\}\), \(y\in\{\tfrac32,\tfrac52\}\). Because \(f\) is a product, the sum is \(2(1+3)\left(\tfrac94+\tfrac{25}4\right)=2\cdot4\cdot\tfrac{17}{2}=68\).
  4. 04

    Each statement claims a value or sign for an integral over a rectangle.

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[-1,1]\times[0,2]}(x+y)\,dA=\iint_{[-1,1]\times[0,2]}y\,dA\).
    2. (b)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[-2,2]\times[-1,1]}x^3\cos y\,dA=0\).
    3. (c)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[-1,1]\times[-1,1]}\left(x^3+1\right)dA=0\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[-1,1]\times[-1,1]}x^2y^2\,dA=0\) because the square is symmetric about the origin.
    5. (e)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_{[0,\pi]\times[0,\pi]}\sin(x+y)\,dA=0\).
    Solution to problem 4

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Theorem symmetry (odd integrand)

      Split the integral. The \(x\) term is odd in \(x\) over the symmetric interval \([-1,1]\), so it contributes \(0\). Both sides equal \(4\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Theorem symmetry (odd integrand)

      The integrand is odd in \(x\) and the \(x\)-interval \([-2,2]\) is symmetric about \(0\), so the contributions from \(x<0\) and \(x>0\) cancel. (Or: \(\int_{-2}^2x^3\,dx=0\) is one factor of a product.)
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Theorem symmetry (odd integrand)

      Only the \(x^3\) part cancels. The constant \(1\) integrates to the area of the square, so the value is \(4\).
    4. (d)

      (d) False. Theorem symmetry (even integrand)

      A symmetric region only forces cancellation when the integrand is odd. Here \(x^2y^2\ge0\) is even, and the value is \(\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac49\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi\sin(x+y)\,dx=\cos y-\cos(\pi+y)=2\cos y\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi2\cos y\,dy=0\). (Note \(x+y\) runs over \([0,2\pi]\), where sine takes both signs.)
  5. 05

    Each statement claims the value of an iterated integral over a rectangle.

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1e^{2x+3y}\,dx\,dy=\frac{e^5-1}{6}\).
    2. (b)
      \(\displaystyle\int_1^e\!\!\int_0^2\frac{x}{y}\,dx\,dy=2\).
    3. (c)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\!\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos(x+y)\,dx\,dy=0\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2(x+y)^2\,dy\,dx=\left(\int_0^1x^2\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^2y^2\,dy\right)\).
    5. (e)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^3 y(x-y)\,dx\,dy=1\).
    Solution to problem 5

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) False. Calculation

      Write \(e^{2x+3y}=e^{2x}e^{3y}\) and separate: \(\displaystyle\left(\int_0^1e^{2x}\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^1e^{3y}\,dy\right)=\frac{(e^2-1)(e^3-1)}{6}\), which is not \(\frac{e^5-1}{6}\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      \(\displaystyle\left(\int_0^2x\,dx\right)\left(\int_1^e\frac{dy}{y}\right)=2\cdot1=2\).
    3. (c)

      (c) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\sin\!\left(y+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)-\sin y=\cos y-\sin y\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}(\cos y-\sin y)\,dy=1-1=0\).
    4. (d)

      (d) False. Theorem Fubini's theorem (product integrands)

      An integral over a rectangle splits into a product only when the integrand is a product \(g(x)h(y)\). \((x+y)^2\neq x^2y^2\). The left side is \(\tfrac{16}{3}\); the right side is \(\tfrac89\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_0^3(xy-y^2)\,dx=\tfrac92y-3y^2\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\left(\tfrac92y-3y^2\right)dy=9-8=1\).
  6. 06

    The following statements concern average value, volume, and basic properties of double integrals.

    1. (a)
      The average value of \(f(x,y)=x+y\) on \([0,2]\times[0,4]\) is \(3\).
    2. (b)
      The average value of \(f(x,y)=\sin x\sin y\) on \([0,\pi]\times[0,\pi]\) is \(\dfrac{4}{\pi^2}\).
    3. (c)
      If \(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA=0\), then \(f(x,y)=0\) at every point of \(R\).
    4. (d)
      The volume of the solid under \(z=4-x-y\) and above \([0,1]\times[0,2]\) is \(5\).
    5. (e)
      If \(f(x,y)\ge2\) on a rectangle \(R\) of area \(5\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_R f\,dA\ge10\).
    Solution to problem 6

    Key: four statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Definition average value

      \(\displaystyle\iint(x+y)\,dA=\int_0^2(4x+8)\,dx=24\), and the area is \(8\), so the average is \(\frac{24}{8}=3\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Definition average value

      The integral is \(\left(\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx\right)^2=4\), and the area is \(\pi^2\).
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Theorem signed volume

      The double integral is a signed volume; positive and negative parts can cancel. For example \(\displaystyle\iint_{[-1,1]\times[0,1]}x\,dA=0\) but \(x\neq0\).
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Calculation

      On the rectangle \(z\ge4-1-2=1>0\), so the volume is the integral: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2(4-x-y)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1(6-2x)\,dx=5\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Theorem comparison property

      If \(f\ge g\) on \(R\), then \(\iint_R f\,dA\ge\iint_R g\,dA\). With \(g=2\), the right side is \(2\cdot\operatorname{Area}(R)=10\).
  7. 07

    The following statements concern the order of integration over a rectangle.

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^2\!\!\int_0^1xe^{xy}\,dx\,dy=\frac{e^2-3}{2}\).
    2. (b)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2xe^{xy}\,dy\,dx=\frac{e^2-3}{2}\).
    3. (c)
      If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\times[c,d]\), then \(\displaystyle\int_a^b\!\!\int_c^d f\,dy\,dx=\int_c^d\!\!\int_a^b f\,dx\,dy\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^{\ln2}\!\!\int_0^{\ln3}e^{x+y}\,dx\,dy=6\).
    5. (e)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^1 6x^2y\,dx\,dy=\left(\int_0^3 6x^2\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^1y\,dy\right)\).
    Solution to problem 7

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Theorem Fubini's theorem

      This is the same rectangle \([0,1]\times[0,2]\) in the other order, so by Fubini's theorem it has the same value as integrating in \(y\) first. (Doing it in this order directly needs integration by parts, which is why the other order is the smart choice.)
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      Integrate in \(y\) first: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2xe^{xy}\,dy=\big[e^{xy}\big]_{y=0}^{2}=e^{2x}-1\). Then \(\displaystyle\int_0^1(e^{2x}-1)\,dx=\frac{e^2-1}{2}-1=\frac{e^2-3}{2}\).
    3. (c)

      (c) True. Theorem Fubini's theorem

      This is Fubini's theorem. Note that each pair of limits stays with its own variable.
    4. (d)

      (d) False. Calculation

      \(\displaystyle\left(\int_0^{\ln3}e^x\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^{\ln2}e^y\,dy\right)=(3-1)(2-1)=2\). The value \(6=e^{\ln3}e^{\ln2}\) forgets to subtract the lower limits.
    5. (e)

      (e) False. Theorem Fubini's theorem (product integrands)

      The product splits, but the limits must stay with their variables: \(x\in[0,1]\) and \(y\in[0,3]\). Correctly, \(\left(\int_0^1 6x^2\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^3y\,dy\right)=2\cdot\tfrac92=9\); the right side shown is \(27\).

General regions & order of integration

Problems 8–15
  1. 08

    Let \(D\) be the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((2,2)\), and \((2,4)\).

    1. (a)
      \(\operatorname{Area}(D)=2\).
    2. (b)
      The average value of \(x\) over \(D\) is \(1\).
    3. (c)
      \(D=\{(x,y)\mid 0\le x\le 2,\ x\le y\le 2x\}\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\iint_D 6ye^x\,dA=18(e^2-1)\).
    5. (e)
      For any continuous \(f\), \(\displaystyle\iint_D f\,dA=\int_0^2\!\!\int_{y/2}^{y}f\,dx\,dy+\int_2^4\!\!\int_{y/2}^{2}f\,dx\,dy\).
    Solution to problem 8

    Key: four statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Calculation

      \(\displaystyle\int_0^2(2x-x)\,dx=2\). Geometrically: base \(2\) along \(x=2\), height \(2\).
    2. (b)

      (b) False. Definition average value

      \(\displaystyle\iint_D x\,dA=\int_0^2 x\cdot x\,dx=\frac83\), so the average is \(\frac{8/3}{2}=\frac43\). The triangle is taller on the right, which pulls the average past the midpoint \(x=1\).
    3. (c)

      (c) True. Calculation

      The lower edge is \(y=x\) (through \((0,0)\) and \((2,2)\)), the upper edge is \(y=2x\) (through \((0,0)\) and \((2,4)\)), and the right edge is \(x=2\).
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_x^{2x}6y\,dy=3(4x^2-x^2)=9x^2\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^2 9x^2e^x\,dx=9\big[(x^2-2x+2)e^x\big]_0^2=9(2e^2-2)=18(e^2-1)\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Calculation

      Slicing horizontally, the left boundary is always \(x=y/2\), but the right boundary changes at \(y=2\): it is \(x=y\) below and \(x=2\) above. That change forces two integrals.
  2. 09

    Let \(\displaystyle I=\int_0^9\!\!\int_{\sqrt{x}}^{3}\cos\!\left(y^3\right)dy\,dx\).

    1. (a)
      The region of integration is bounded by the parabola \(x=y^2\), the line \(y=3\), and the \(x\)-axis.
    2. (b)
      The inner integral \(\displaystyle\int_{\sqrt x}^{3}\cos\!\left(y^3\right)dy\) can be evaluated with the substitution \(u=y^3\).
    3. (c)
      \(\displaystyle I=\int_0^3\!\!\int_{y^2}^{9}\cos\!\left(y^3\right)dx\,dy\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle I=\int_0^3\!\!\int_0^{y^2}\cos\!\left(y^3\right)dx\,dy\).
    5. (e)
      \(I=\sin(27)\).
    Solution to problem 9

    Key: one statement is true.

    1. (a)

      (a) False. Calculation

      The third boundary is the \(y\)-axis \(x=0\). The region only touches the \(x\)-axis at the origin. Sketching before reversing is what catches this.
    2. (b)

      (b) False. Calculation

      There is no factor of \(y^2\) to absorb \(du=3y^2\,dy\), and \(\cos(y^3)\) has no elementary antiderivative. That is exactly why the order must be reversed.
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Calculation

      This describes the region to the right of the parabola, that is, below the curve \(y=\sqrt x\), which is the complement of the given region in \([0,9]\times[0,3]\).
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Calculation

      The region is \(0\le x\le 9\), \(\sqrt x\le y\le 3\). For a fixed \(y\in[0,3]\), \(x\) runs from the \(y\)-axis to the parabola \(x=y^2\).
    5. (e)

      (e) False. Calculation

      After reversing, the inner integral gives \(y^2\cos(y^3)\), and \(\displaystyle\int_0^3 y^2\cos(y^3)\,dy=\tfrac13\sin(y^3)\Big|_0^3=\tfrac13\sin 27\). The answer \(\sin 27\) drops the \(\tfrac13\).
  3. 10

    Each statement concerns a double integral over a region \(D\) described by inequalities.

    1. (a)
      The regions \(\{0\le y\le1,\ y^2\le x\le y\}\) and \(\{0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le x\}\) are the same.
    2. (b)
      If \(D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le x\le1,\ x^2\le y\le x\}\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_D2x\,dA=\frac16\).
    3. (c)
      If \(D=\{(x,y)\mid1\le x\le e,\ 0\le y\le1/x\}\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_D x\,dA=\int_1^e\!\!\int_0^{1/x}x\,dx\,dy\).
    4. (d)
      If \(D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le x\le\pi,\ 0\le y\le x\}\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_D\sin x\,dA=\pi\).
    5. (e)
      If \(D=\{(x,y)\mid0\le x\le1,\ 1-x\le y\le2-x\}\), then \(\displaystyle\iint_D e^{x+y}\,dA=e^2-e\).
    Solution to problem 10

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) False. Calculation

      The first is between \(x=y^2\) and \(x=y\), so it lies above the line \(y=x\) (it is \(x\le y\le\sqrt x\)). The second lies below \(y=x\). They are mirror images across \(y=x\), not the same region.
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1 2x\left(x-x^2\right)dx=2\left(\tfrac13-\tfrac14\right)=\tfrac16\).
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Definition iterated integral

      The inner limits may depend only on the outer variable. Here the inner variable is \(x\) but its upper limit is \(1/x\), which makes no sense. Correct: \(\displaystyle\int_1^e\!\!\int_0^{1/x}x\,dy\,dx=e-1\).
    4. (d)

      (d) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_0^x\sin x\,dy=x\sin x\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^\pi x\sin x\,dx=\big[-x\cos x+\sin x\big]_0^\pi=\pi\).
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Calculation

      Inner: \(\displaystyle\int_{1-x}^{2-x}e^{x+y}\,dy=e^{2}-e^{1}\), a constant because \(x+y\) runs from \(1\) to \(2\). Outer: \(\displaystyle\int_0^1(e^2-e)\,dx=e^2-e\).
  4. 11

    Each statement claims an equality obtained by changing the order of integration. Assume \(f\) and \(g\) are continuous.

    1. (a)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^yg(x)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1(1-x)\,g(x)\,dx\).
    2. (b)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_y^1f\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^xf\,dy\,dx\).
    3. (c)
      \(\displaystyle\int_{-2}^{2}\!\!\int_0^{4-x^2}f\,dy\,dx=\int_0^4\!\!\int_{-\sqrt{4-y}}^{\sqrt{4-y}}f\,dx\,dy\).
    4. (d)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^4\!\!\int_{\sqrt x}^2f\,dy\,dx=\int_0^2\!\!\int_{y^2}^4f\,dx\,dy\).
    5. (e)
      \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1e^{y^2}\,dy\,dx=\frac{e-1}{2}\).
    Solution to problem 11

    Key: four statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Calculation

      The region is \(0\le x\le y\le1\). In the order \(dy\,dx\): \(\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1g(x)\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1g(x)(1-x)\,dx\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Calculation

      Both describe the triangle \(0\le y\le x\le1\).
    3. (c)

      (c) True. Calculation

    Key: three statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Formula polar conversion

      Multiply through: \(2r\sin\theta+3r\cos\theta=6\), so \(2y+3x=6\), or \(y=3-\tfrac32x\).

      (b) False. Formula polar conversion

      \(r^2=4r\sin\theta\) gives \(x^2+y^2=4y\), so \(x^2+(y-2)^2=4\): radius \(2\), center \((0,2)\).

      (c) False. Calculation

      The condition \(r\ge 1\) removes the inner quarter-disk, leaving a quarter of an annulus.

      (d) True. Definition rose curve \(r=\sin n\theta\)

      For \(r=\sin n\theta\) with \(n\) odd, the rose has \(n\) petals.
    5. (e)

      (e) True. Formula polar conversion

      On that range \(\sin\theta\ge\frac{\sqrt2}{2}>0\), so \(y=r\sin\theta\ge 0\). It is a sector of radius \(2\) opening upward.

      The spherical map \((\rho,\phi,\theta)\mapsto(x,y,z)\) has \(\abs{\operatorname{Jac}}=\rho\sin\phi\).
    4. (d)
      If \(\operatorname{Jac}(T)<0\), then the area of \(T(D)\) is negative.
    5. (e)
      The image of the unit square under \(T(u,v)=(u+v,\ u-v)\) has area \(1\).
    Solution to problem 48

    Key: two statements are true.

    1. (a)

      (a) True. Definition Jacobian

      \(\begin{vmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{vmatrix}=r\).
    2. (b)

      (b) True. Theorem area scaling by the Jacobian

      \(\operatorname{Jac}(T)=\begin{vmatrix}3&0\\0&3\end{vmatrix}=9\).
    3. (c)

      (c) False. Formula volume element in spherical

      It is \(\rho^2\sin\phi\), which is where \(dV=\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta\) comes from.
    4. (d)

      (d) False. Theorem change of variables

      Area scales by \(\abs{\operatorname{Jac}(T)}\). A negative Jacobian only means the map reverses orientation.
    5. (e)

      (e) False. Theorem area scaling by the Jacobian

      \(\operatorname{Jac}(T)=\begin{vmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-2\), so the image has area \(2\).
